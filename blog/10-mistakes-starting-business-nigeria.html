10 Mistakes to Avoid When Starting a Business in Nigeria

By BusinessMen Editorial 7 min read Updated May 20, 2026

Starting a business in Nigeria is exciting. You finally get to be your own boss, set your own hours, and build something from nothing. But excitement alone won't keep your doors open. I've watched too many promising businesses fail within their first year because the founders made avoidable mistakes.

Here are the ten biggest mistakes I see Nigerian entrepreneurs make, registering your business properlyand more importantly, how you can dodge every single one of them.

1. Not Doing Proper Market Research

Too many people start businesses because "everyone needs this" without actually verifying that people will pay for it. Your friend in Abuja might love your idea, but that doesn't mean there's a market.

Before you spend a single naira, talk to potential customers. Not your family members who will tell you everything is great to avoid hurting your feelings. Real strangers who would actually spend money on your product or service.

Walk around the area where you plan to operate. Count your competitors. Visit their shops. See how busy they are. Ask their customers what they like and what they wish was different. This costs nothing but time, and it's the most valuable research you'll ever do.

2. Mixing Personal and Business Money

This is probably the most common mistake I see. You open a business, and the first sale comes in. Instead of keeping that money in the business, you use it to buy groceries, pay your child's school fees, or settle a personal debt. Then when you need to restock inventory, there's nothing left.

Open a separate bank account for your business immediately after registration. Every sale goes in. Every business expense comes out. Pay yourself a fixed salary from the profits. This discipline is what separates businesses that survive from ones that collapse within months.

3. Underestimating Startup Costs

You budgeted ₦500,000 to open your small provision store. But by the time you pay for shop rent (₦150,000 for the year), shelves (₦80,000), initial stock (₦200,000), CAC registration (₦25,000), a generator (₦120,000 because NEPA won't be reliable), and miscellaneous expenses, you've spent ₦575,000 and you haven't even opened your doors yet.

Always add 30% to your estimated startup costs. Things will cost more than you think. They always do in Nigeria. Fuel prices change. Rent increases. Suppliers raise prices. Build a buffer into your budget so you're not scrambling for cash in your first month.

4. Choosing the Wrong Location

Location can make or break your business. I know a guy who opened a high-end fashion boutique on a street in Yaba where nobody walks with disposable income. Beautiful shop, great products, zero foot traffic. He relocated to Lekki six months later and his sales tripled.

Don't choose a location just because the rent is cheap. Choose it because your target customers are there. A luxury salon belongs in Ikoyi or Maitama, not in a residential area where people are counting every naira. A budget eatery thrives near universities and motor parks, not in a highbrow estate.

5. Not Registering the Business

I mentioned this in another post, but it bears repeating. Operating without CAC registration limits your growth. You can't open a proper corporate bank account. You can't bid for government contracts. You can't get most business loans. And someone can register your business name and legally force you to change it.

Registration costs between ₦20,000 and ₦50,000 for a business name. That's a small price to pay for legitimacy and protection. Do it early.

6. Hiring the Wrong People

Your first employees will shape your business culture. Hire someone dishonest, and they'll steal from you. Hire someone lazy, and they'll drive away your customers. Hire someone who doesn't share your vision, and they'll do the bare minimum.

Take time with hiring. Check references. Do a trial period. Pay fairly but tie bonuses to performance. And never, ever hire just because someone is your cousin or your pastor recommended them. Hire based on competence and character, period.

7. Ignoring Online Presence

It's 2026. If your business doesn't exist online, it doesn't exist. Period. Your customers are on Instagram. They're searching on Google. They're checking business directories before they visit a new shop.

8. No Pricing Strategy

Some entrepreneurs price by guessing. Others price by copying competitors without understanding their cost structure. Both approaches are dangerous.

Your price needs to cover your costs (materials, rent, salaries, utilities, transportation) and leave enough profit to sustain and grow the business. Calculate your cost per unit or per service, add your desired profit margin, and that's your price. Then compare it with competitors to make sure you're in the right ballpark.

Don't engage in a race to the bottom on price. There will always be someone willing to charge less. Compete on quality, customer service, and reliability instead.

9. Not Planning for the Dry Season

Every business has good months and bad months. A restaurant might do well during December and struggle in February. A fashion brand might fly during festive seasons and crawl during rainy months. A construction company might slow down during heavy rains.

Plan for this. Save during your good months so you can survive the bad ones. Don't expand your expenses just because you had three great months in a row. Keep your overhead lean and build a cash reserve that can cover at least three months of operating expenses.

10. Trying to Do Everything Alone

The lone wolf entrepreneur is a myth. Yes, you might start alone. But you can't handle marketing, accounting, customer service, product development, and delivery all by yourself forever. You'll burn out, and your business will suffer.

Build a network. Join entrepreneur groups in your city. Find a mentor who's been where you want to go. Outsource tasks that aren't your strength. Hire help when you can afford it. Partner with complementary businesses. The Nigerian business community is full of people willing to help if you're willing to ask.

The Bottom Line

Starting a business in Nigeria isn't easy, but it's far from impossible. Thousands of people do it successfully every year. The difference between those who succeed and those who fail often comes down to avoiding these basic mistakes.

Do your research. Keep your finances clean. Register your business. Choose your location wisely. Hire carefully. Get online. Price smartly. Plan for lean months. And don't try to do everything alone.

If you can avoid these ten mistakes, you'll already be ahead of most new business owners in Nigeria. Now go build something great. And when you're ready for customers to find you, make sure you're listed on BusinessMen.
