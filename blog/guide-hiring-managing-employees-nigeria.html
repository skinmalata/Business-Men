A Guide to Hiring and Managing Employees in Nigeria

By BusinessMen Editorial 8 min read Updated May 20, 2026

Hiring your first employee is a milestone. It means your business has grown beyond what you can handle alone. But it also means new responsibilities: payroll, employment law, performance management, and the very real possibility that a bad hire can damage your business.

Understanding Nigerian Employment Law

The primary legislation governing employment in Nigeria is the Labour Act (Cap L1, Laws of the Federation of Nigeria 2004). Here are the key provisions every employer should know:

Employment Contracts

The Labour Act requires that every employee receive a written contract within three months of starting work. The contract should include:

  • Names of employer and employee
  • Job title and description
  • Start date
  • Salary and payment frequency
  • Working hours
  • Leave entitlements
  • Notice period for termination
  • Any other terms and conditions

Don't rely on verbal agreements. A written contract protects both you and the employee. If things go wrong, it's your first line of defense.

Working Hours and Overtime

The standard working week in Nigeria is 40 hours (8 hours per day, 5 days a week). Any work beyond this is considered overtime and should be compensated. The Labour Act doesn't specify an overtime rate, so it's negotiable. Common practice is 1.5x the normal hourly rate for overtime on regular days and 2x for weekends and public holidays.

Leave Entitlements

Employees who have worked for at least 12 continuous months are entitled to a minimum of 6 working days of paid annual leave. Many companies offer more (10-20 days is common). Sick leave is typically 7-14 days with full pay, subject to a medical certificate.

Maternity leave is 12 weeks (6 weeks before and 6 weeks after delivery) with at least 50% pay. This applies to women who have worked for at least 6 months and have notified the employer in writing.

Termination and Notice Periods

The notice period depends on the length of employment:

  • Less than 3 months: 1 day notice
  • 3 months to 2 years: 1 week notice
  • 2 to 5 years: 2 weeks notice
  • 5 years and above: 1 month notice

You can terminate without notice for gross misconduct (theft, fraud, violence), but you need to follow due process. Summary dismissal without proper cause can lead to legal action.

Minimum Wage

Nigeria's national minimum wage was revised to ₦70,000 in 2024. This is the legal minimum for all workers. However, many states and sectors have their own arrangements. Always pay at least the minimum wage, and ideally more if you want to attract and retain good talent.

The Hiring Process

Step 1: Define the Role

Before you advertise, know exactly what you need. Write a clear job description with specific responsibilities, required qualifications, and the skills you're looking for. Don't just say "we need a marketer." Say "we need someone who can manage our Instagram account, create content calendars, run Facebook ads with a monthly budget of ₦200,000, and report on engagement metrics weekly."

Step 2: Advertise the Position

Post your job on platforms like LinkedIn, Jobberman, and Hot Nigerian Jobs. You can also share on your social media pages. For entry-level positions, word of mouth and referrals from existing staff work well.

Be clear about the salary range. "Negotiable" attracts too many unqualified applicants and wastes everyone's time.

Step 3: Screen and Interview

Review applications carefully. Check that candidates meet the minimum requirements. Then conduct interviews. For important roles, do at least two rounds: one to assess skills and another to evaluate cultural fit.

Ask behavioral questions: "Tell me about a time you handled a difficult customer." "Describe a project you managed from start to finish." These reveal more than "Where do you see yourself in five years?"

Step 4: Verify References

This step is often skipped in Nigeria, and it shouldn't be. Call the candidate's previous employers. Ask about their reliability, honesty, work quality, and reason for leaving. A 10-minute phone call can save you months of headaches.

Step 5: Make an Offer

Once you've chosen your candidate, send a formal offer letter with the salary, start date, and key terms. Give them a reasonable deadline to accept (usually 3-5 business days).

Payroll and Statutory Deductions

As an employer, you're responsible for:

  • PAYE (Pay As You Earn): Deduct personal income tax from employee salaries and remit to your state's internal revenue service monthly.
  • Pension: Under the Pension Reform Act 2014, employers with 3 or more employees must contribute to a pension fund. The employer contributes 10% and the employee contributes 8% of the employee's monthly emolument.
  • NSITF: The Nigeria Social Insurance Trust Fund requires a 1% employer contribution for employee compensation.
  • ITF: The Industrial Training Fund requires a 1% contribution from companies with 5 or more employees or annual turnover above ₦50 million.

Use payroll software or hire an accountant to handle these deductions. Getting them wrong can result in penalties.

Managing Your Employees

Set Clear Expectations

From day one, make sure your employee knows what's expected of them. What are their key responsibilities? What does success look like in their role? How will their performance be measured? Write it down and review it together.

Communicate Regularly

Don't wait for annual reviews to give feedback. Have weekly or bi-weekly check-ins. Praise good work. Address issues early before they become big problems. Nigerian employees appreciate bosses who communicate openly and treat them with respect.

Invest in Training

Your employees are only as good as their skills. Invest in training. Send them to workshops. Pay for online courses. Bring in experts to train your team. The money you spend on training pays for itself in improved productivity and reduced errors.

Create a Positive Work Environment

This doesn't mean a fancy office with a foosball table. It means treating people with respect, paying them on time, listening to their concerns, and creating a workplace where people feel valued. In Nigeria, where job security is a major concern, a stable and supportive work environment goes a long way in retaining good employees.

Handle Discipline Fairly

When an employee messes up, follow a fair process:

  1. Investigate the issue thoroughly.
  2. Give the employee a chance to explain their side.
  3. Apply consequences proportionally to the offense.
  4. Document everything.

Don't skip steps. Even if you're right, a unfair process can come back to haunt you legally.

Common Hiring Mistakes in Nigeria

Hiring based on connections rather than competence. Your pastor's recommendation doesn't mean the person can do the job. Hire for skills and character.

Underpaying to save money. Cheap employees cost more in the long run through mistakes, low productivity, and high turnover. Pay fairly and get quality.

No onboarding process. Throwing a new employee into the deep end on their first day is a recipe for confusion and frustration. Have a structured onboarding plan.

Not having an employee handbook. Even a simple one-page document outlining your company policies, working hours, leave procedures, and code of conduct is better than nothing.

Final Thoughts

Hiring and managing employees is one of the most important skills you'll develop as a business owner. Get it right, and your team becomes your greatest competitive advantage. Get it wrong, and even the best business idea will struggle.

Follow the law, treat people well, communicate clearly, and invest in your team. The returns will show up in your bottom line.
