Understanding Nigerian Tax Laws for Small Business Owners

By BusinessMen Editorial 9 min read Updated May 20, 2026

Taxes. Nobody likes talking about them, and even fewer people enjoy paying them. But if you're running a business in Nigeria, understanding your tax obligations isn't optional. It's the difference between sleeping peacefully at night and getting a nasty letter from the tax authorities.

I'm going to break down Nigerian tax laws in plain English. No jargon, no registering your choosing the right business bank accountbusiness and getting a TINconfusing legal language. Just what you need to know as a small business owner.

Who Collects Taxes in Nigeria?

First, you need to know who you're dealing with. Nigeria has a three-tier tax system:

  • Federal Inland Revenue Service (FIRS): Collects federal taxes like Company Income Tax, VAT, and Education Tax. They handle businesses registered at the federal level.
  • State Internal Revenue Services: Each state has its own revenue service (Lagos has LIRS, Abuja has FCT-IRS, etc.). They collect Personal Income Tax, Withholding Tax (for individuals), and various state levies.
  • Local Government Revenue Committees: They collect local levies like shop permits, signage fees, and market taxes.

Yes, it's complicated. Yes, it feels like everybody wants a piece of your money. But understanding who collects what helps you avoid paying the same tax twice or missing an obligation entirely.

Your Tax Identification Number (TIN)

When you register your business with the CAC, you automatically get a Tax Identification Number. This is your tax ID, and you'll need it for everything from filing returns to opening a corporate bank account.

If for some reason you don't have a TIN, you can apply for one through the FIRS website or visit your nearest tax office. Don't operate without one. It's like driving without a license. Sooner or later, it catches up with you.

Types of Taxes Your Business Might Owe

Company Income Tax (CIT)

If you registered a limited liability company, you pay Company Income Tax on your profits. The rate is 30% for large companies, 20% for medium companies (annual turnover between ₦25 million and ₦100 million), and 0% for small companies (annual turnover below ₦25 million).

That zero percent for small companies is a big deal. The Finance Act introduced this to support small businesses. If your annual turnover is under ₦25 million, you're exempt from CIT. But you still need to file your returns. Exemption doesn't mean you ignore the process.

Value Added Tax (VAT)

VAT is currently 7.5% on most goods and services. When you sell something, you add 7.5% to the price and collect it from the customer. Then you remit that VAT to the FIRS.

Here's the thing: some items are exempt from VAT. Basic food items, medical and pharmaceutical products, educational materials, and residential rent are all VAT-exempt. If your business deals in these items, you don't charge VAT.

But if you sell electronics, clothing, consulting services, or restaurant meals, you charge VAT. And you need to file monthly VAT returns, even if you had no sales in a particular month. File a nil return rather than not filing at all.

Personal Income Tax (PAYE)

If you have employees, you're responsible for deducting Pay As You Earn (PAYE) tax from their salaries and remitting it to your state's internal revenue service. The tax rates are progressive, meaning higher earners pay a higher percentage.

The current PAYE rates range from 7% to 24% depending on income level. The first ₦300,000 of annual income is taxed at 7%, and income above ₦3,200,000 is taxed at 24%. Your state revenue service can provide you with a detailed tax table.

Even if you're a sole proprietor, you pay personal income tax on your business profits. This goes to your state revenue service, not FIRS.

Education Tax

Companies pay 2% of their assessable profit as Education Tax. This goes to the Tertiary Education Trust Fund (TETFund). It's calculated alongside your Company Income Tax.

Withholding Tax

Withholding Tax (WHT) is like a prepayment of income tax. When a company pays you for services, they're supposed to deduct a percentage (usually 5% or 10%) and remit it to the tax authority on your behalf. You get a WHT credit note, which you can use to offset your income tax liability.

As a business owner, you also need to deduct WHT when you pay contractors, consultants, and certain service providers. Keep track of all WHT credits. They're essentially money you've already paid toward your taxes.

Filing Your Tax Returns

Here's the calendar you need to know:

  • VAT returns: Filed monthly, due by the 21st of the following month.
  • Company Income Tax returns: Filed annually within six months of your financial year-end. Most companies use December 31 as their year-end, so returns are due by June 30.
  • PAYE: Remitted monthly, usually by the 10th of the following month.
  • Personal Income Tax (for sole proprietors): Filed annually by March 31.

Missing deadlines attracts penalties. The FIRS isn't playing around anymore. They've been upgrading their systems and they're getting better at tracking non-compliant businesses. It's cheaper and less stressful to file on time.

Tax Incentives for Small Businesses

It's not all about paying. The Nigerian government offers some tax incentives that you should know about:

  • Small company CIT exemption: As mentioned, companies with turnover below ₦25 million pay 0% CIT.
  • Pioneer status: If your business is in an industry designated as "pioneer," you can get a tax holiday of 3 to 5 years.
  • Rural investment allowance: Businesses located in rural areas can claim additional capital allowances.
  • Export expansion grant: If you export goods, there are tax benefits available through the Nigerian Export Promotion Council.

Common Tax Mistakes to Avoid

Not filing nil returns. Even if you made no profit or had no sales in a period, you still need to file. A nil return is better than no return.

Mixing up federal and state taxes. CIT goes to FIRS. Personal income tax goes to your state. Don't pay your state revenue service when you owe FIRS, or vice versa.

Not keeping proper records. You need receipts, invoices, bank statements, and financial statements to support your tax filings. If the tax authorities audit you and you can't produce records, they'll assess you based on their estimates, which are usually higher than what you'd actually owe.

Ignoring local government levies. Yes, they're annoying. Yes, some of them feel excessive. But unpaid local government levies can lead to shop closures and harassment. Know what you owe and pay what's legally required.

Should You Hire a Tax Consultant?

For a very small business, you might be able to handle your taxes yourself, especially with the FIRS's online filing system. But as your business grows, or if your tax situation is complex (multiple revenue streams, imports, exports), hiring a tax consultant or accountant is worth the investment.

A good tax consultant doesn't just file your returns. They help you plan your taxes legally to minimize what you pay. They know the incentives you qualify for. They keep you updated on changes in tax law. And they represent you if the tax authorities come knocking.

Expect to pay between ₦30,000 and ₦150,000 annually for basic tax consulting services for a small business. Compare that to the penalties for non-compliance, which can run into hundreds of thousands of naira, and it's a bargain.

Final Thoughts

Taxes are a fact of doing business. But they don't have to be a mystery. Understand your obligations, keep good records, file on time, and take advantage of the incentives available to you. The Nigerian tax system has gotten more streamlined in recent years, and the FIRS has made online filing much easier.

Treat your tax obligations the same way you treat your customers. With respect, attention, and consistency. Do that, and you'll never have to worry about the tax authorities disrupting your business.
