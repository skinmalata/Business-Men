How to Write a Business Plan for a Nigerian Startup

By BusinessMen Editorial 8 min read Updated May 20, 2026

You've got a brilliant idea. Maybe it's a food delivery app that works better than the ones currently in Lagos. Maybe it's a solar energy solution for rural communities in Kaduna. Maybe it's a fashion brand that combines traditional Ankara with modern designs.

Great. But ideas don't get funding. Business plans do.

Whether you're approaching an investor in Victoria Island, applying for a Bank of Industry loan, or just trying to figure out if your idea actually makes financial registering your finding investors for your startupbusiness with the CACsense, you need a solid business plan. And not one of those generic templates you downloaded from the internet. You need something that speaks to the Nigerian market.

What Exactly Is a Business Plan?

A business plan is a written document that describes what your business does, who it serves, how it makes money, and how it plans to grow. Think of it as a roadmap. Without it, you're driving from Lagos to Abuja without Google Maps. You might eventually get there, but you'll waste a lot of fuel and time making wrong turns.

Your business plan serves two main purposes:

  • Internal: It helps you think through every aspect of your business before you spend money.
  • External: It convinces investors, banks, and partners that your business is worth their time and money.

The Essential Sections of a Nigerian Business Plan

1. Executive Summary

This is the first thing anyone reads, so it needs to be sharp. Write it last, even though it appears first. It should summarize your entire plan in one to two pages.

Include your business name, location, what you do, who your target market is, how much money you need (if you're seeking funding), and what makes you mistakes to avoid as a new entrepreneurdifferent. Keep it tight. Investors in Lagos see hundreds of business plans. If your executive summary doesn't grab them in the first paragraph, they'll move on.

2. Company Description

Here's where you explain what your business is and what problem it solves. Be specific. Don't say "we provide technology solutions." Say "we provide inventory management software for small pharmacies in Nigeria that currently use paper records."

Include your CAC registration status, your business structure (sole proprietorship, partnership, or limited company), and your mission statement. If you've already started operations, mention your traction. How many customers do you have? What revenue have you generated?

3. Market Analysis

This is where many Nigerian entrepreneurs fall short. They say things like "everyone in Nigeria is my customer." That's not a market. That's a population.

You need to narrow it down. Who exactly will buy from you? What's the size of that market? Who are your competitors? What are they doing wrong that you'll do better?

Use real data. The National Bureau of Statistics publishes reports. Industry associations release market data. Talk to people in your target market. If you're opening a restaurant in Lekki, go to Lekki and count how many restaurants are on a single street. Talk to their customers. Find out what they like and what they complain about.

For example, if you're building a fintech app, you should know that Nigeria has over 200 million people, about 60% are under 25, smartphone penetration is growing rapidly, and the Central Bank's cashless policy is driving digital payments. These are the facts that make your market analysis credible.

4. Organization and Management

Who's running this business? Investors don't just invest in ideas. They invest in people. Describe your team, their experience, and their roles.

If you're a solo founder, that's fine. But be honest about what skills you lack and how you plan to fill those gaps. Maybe you're great at product development but terrible at sales. Acknowledge it and explain that you plan to hire a sales lead within six months.

Include an organizational chart if your team is more than three people. Show who reports to whom. It doesn't have to be fancy. A simple diagram works.

5. Products or Services

Describe exactly what you're selling. How does it work? What makes it better than what's already available? What's your pricing strategy?

If you're selling physical products, explain your supply chain. Where do you source materials? Who manufactures? How do you handle quality control?

If you're offering a service, explain your delivery process. How do customers access your service? What's the customer experience from start to finish?

Be specific about pricing. Don't say "our prices are competitive." Say "our basic plan costs ₦5,000 per month, which is 30% less than our closest competitor while offering two additional features."

6. Marketing and Sales Strategy

How will customers find you? This is crucial in Nigeria where the market can be fragmented and customer acquisition costs can be surprisingly high.

Detail your marketing channels:

  • Social media: Which platforms? Instagram works well for fashion and food. LinkedIn for B2B services. Twitter for tech and news-related businesses.
  • Word of mouth: Still the most powerful marketing tool in Nigeria. How will you encourage referrals?
  • Partnerships: Are there complementary businesses you can partner with?
  • Online directories: Listing on platforms like BusinessMen helps customers discover you organically.
  • Traditional media: Radio still works incredibly well in Nigeria, especially outside Lagos and Abuja.

For sales, explain your sales process. Do you sell directly? Through distributors? Online? In physical stores? What's your sales target for the first year?

7. Financial Projections

This is the section investors scrutinize the most. You need:

  • Startup costs: Everything you need to spend before you make your first sale. Registration, equipment, inventory, website, initial marketing.
  • Revenue projections: How much you expect to earn monthly for at least the first year, then annually for years two through five.
  • Expense projections: Rent, salaries, utilities, marketing, inventory restocking, transportation.
  • Break-even analysis: When will your revenue cover all your expenses?
  • Cash flow statement: Money in versus money out, month by month.

Be realistic. Don't project ₦50 million in revenue in your first year if you're opening a single tailoring shop in Ibadan. But don't be too conservative either. Show that you understand the market opportunity.

Here's a practical example: if you're opening a small restaurant in Surulere with 20 seats, and you estimate 40 customers per day spending an average of ₦3,500 each, that's ₦140,000 daily or roughly ₦4.2 million monthly. Now subtract your costs (food, rent at maybe ₦300,000, staff salaries, utilities, etc.) and see what's left.

8. Funding Request (If Applicable)

If you're seeking investment, be clear about how much you need and what you'll use it for. Investors want to see that you've thought about every naira.

Break it down: ₦2 million for equipment, ₦1 million for initial inventory, ₦500,000 for marketing, ₦300,000 for working capital. Specificity builds confidence.

Also explain what the investor gets in return. Are you offering equity? If so, how much? Are you seeking a loan? What's your repayment plan?

Common Business Plan Mistakes Nigerian Entrepreneurs Make

Overestimating the market. Nigeria has 200 million people, but that doesn't mean 200 million people will buy your product. Narrow your target market and be realistic about your share.

Ignoring competition. Saying "we have no competition" is a red flag. There's always competition, even if it's indirect. If you're opening a gym, your competition isn't just other gyms. It's also home workout apps, football viewing centers, and people's general laziness.

Unrealistic financial projections. Projecting that you'll capture 50% of the Nigerian market in year one is a quick way to lose credibility. Start with conservative numbers and show how you'll grow.

No contingency plan. Things go wrong in Nigeria. Fuel prices spike. The naira fluctuates. Supply chains get disrupted. Show that you've thought about these risks and have plans to handle them.

Tools and Resources

You don't need expensive software to write a business plan. A well-structured Word document works perfectly. But these tools can help:

  • Excel or Google Sheets: For financial projections and calculations.
  • Canva: For creating professional-looking charts and graphs.
  • SMEDAN: The Small and Medium Enterprises Development Agency of Nigeria offers free business plan templates and training.
  • Bank of Industry: If you're seeking funding, check their requirements. They have specific business plan formats they prefer.

Final Words

Your business plan is a living document. It's not something you write once and lock in a drawer. Update it as your business grows, as the market changes, and as you learn new things about your customers.

The best business plan is one that you actually use. Refer to it when making decisions. Compare your actual performance against your projections. Adjust your strategy based on what the numbers tell you.
