Tips for Negotiating Better Deals with Suppliers

By BusinessMen Editorial 6 min read Updated May 20, 2026

Every naira you save on your supplies goes straight to your bottom line. If you're buying ₦500,000 worth of goods monthly and you negotiate a 10% discount, that's ₦50,000 saved every month or ₦600,000 per year. That's not small change.

But negotiation isn't about bullying suppliers into giving you the lowest price. It's about creating a deal that works for both sides. Here's how to do it effectively in the Nigerian business context.

Prepare Before You Negotiate

Know the Market Price

Before you sit down with a supplier, know what the going rate is for the products you need. Talk to other suppliers. Check prices at major markets. Search online. If you don't know the market price, you have no basis for negotiation.

In Nigeria, prices can vary significantly between suppliers, especially for imported goods where exchange rate fluctuations affect pricing. Stay updated on current market rates.

Understand Your Leverage

What makes you a valuable customer? Do you buy in large volumes? Do you pay on time? Are you a growing business that will buy more in the future? These are your negotiating chips. Know them and use them.

If you're a small buyer, your leverage is limited. But you can still negotiate on payment terms, delivery schedules, or bundled pricing.

Set Your Target Price

Before the negotiation, decide what price you're aiming for and what your maximum acceptable price is. Having these numbers in mind keeps you focused and prevents you from agreeing to a deal that doesn't work for your business.

Negotiation Strategies That Work

1. Build a Relationship First

In Nigeria, business is personal. Suppliers are more likely to give good deals to people they know and trust. Don't walk into a negotiation cold. Visit the supplier's office. Have a conversation about their business. Show genuine interest.

A supplier who sees you as a long-term partner will be more flexible on pricing than one who sees you as a one-time buyer.

2. Buy in Volume

The most straightforward way to get a better price is to buy more. If you currently buy 100 units monthly, ask what price you'd get for 200 or 500 units. Even if you can't use all the inventory immediately, the savings might justify the larger purchase.

Be careful not to over-order though. Tying up cash in excess inventory can hurt your business more than the savings help it.

3. Offer Faster Payment

Suppliers love customers who pay quickly. If a supplier offers 30-day payment terms, offer to pay within 7 days in exchange for a discount. Many suppliers will give 2-5% off for early payment because it improves their cash flow.

4. Bundle Your Purchases

If you buy multiple products from the same supplier, negotiate a package deal. "I'm buying A, B, and C from you. What's the best price you can give me for all three?" Suppliers often have more flexibility on bundled pricing than on individual items.

5. Be Willing to Walk Away

The most powerful negotiating tool is the willingness to walk away. If a supplier won't budge on price and you've done your research showing the price is above market, be prepared to take your business elsewhere.

Sometimes suppliers will call you back with a better offer after you've walked away. Sometimes they won't. Either way, you'll know you got the best deal possible.

6. Negotiate Beyond Price

Price isn't the only thing you can negotiate. Consider:

  • Payment terms: Can you get 60 days instead of 30?
  • Delivery: Can they deliver for free or at a reduced cost?
  • Minimum order quantities: Can they lower the MOQ so you don't have to tie up as much capital?
  • Return policy: Can you return unsold or defective goods?
  • Exclusivity: Can you get exclusive distribution rights in your area?

Negotiation Mistakes to Avoid

Being too aggressive: Pushing too hard can damage the relationship. Remember, you want this supplier to prioritize your orders when stock is limited. A resentful supplier won't do you any favors.

Accepting the first offer: The first price a supplier quotes is rarely their best price. Always negotiate.

Not getting agreements in writing: Verbal agreements are hard to enforce. Get all negotiated terms in writing, whether it's a formal contract or at least a written quotation with the agreed terms.

Focusing only on price: The cheapest supplier might be the most expensive in the long run if they deliver late, send poor quality goods, or have terrible customer service. Consider the total value, not just the price.

Sharing your budget: Don't tell a supplier how much you're willing to spend. Let them quote first, then negotiate from there.

When to Renegotiate

Don't assume your negotiated price is permanent. Renegotiate when:

  • Your order volume increases significantly
  • Market prices drop
  • You've been a loyal customer for a year or more
  • The supplier raises prices without justification
  • You find a competitor offering better terms

Approach renegotiation as a conversation, not a demand. "We've been buying from you for two years and our volume has doubled. Can we revisit our pricing?" is much more effective than "Your prices are too high."

Final Thoughts

Negotiation is a skill that improves with practice. Don't be afraid to negotiate. Suppliers expect it, and most respect buyers who know what they want and advocate for their business.

The goal isn't to win every negotiation. It's to build relationships with suppliers where both parties feel they're getting a fair deal. Those are the relationships that sustain businesses through thick and thin.
